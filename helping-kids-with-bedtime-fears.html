Helping kids with bedtime fears: gentle tips for parents

Simple, gentle ways to help young children who are scared of the dark or afraid at bedtime: what is normal, what to say, calming routines and picture books that help.

"There's something under my bed!" Almost every parent hears some version of this. Fear of the dark and worries at bedtime are a normal part of growing up, especially between about ages three and seven, when imagination is growing fast. The good news is that there is a lot you can do to help your child feel safe, and most children grow out of these fears with time and gentle support.

Why young children get scared at night

Young children are still learning the difference between what is real and what is pretend. Their imaginations are powerful, and in the dark, a coat on a chair can easily look like a monster. Bedtime is also when children are separated from their grown-ups, which can bring up worries. Changes like a new house, a new sibling or starting school can make nighttime fears stronger for a while.

What to say (and what to avoid)

Listen first

Ask your child to tell you about the fear. "What do you think is in the closet?" Listening shows you take them seriously.

Name the feeling

"You feel scared when the lights go off. That makes sense. Lots of kids feel that way." Naming a feeling helps children understand it.

Be calm and confident

Your calm voice tells your child they are safe. You can say, "I am right down the hall, and our house is safe."

Avoid teasing or dismissing

Saying "Don't be silly" or "Big kids aren't scared" can make children feel ashamed and less likely to share their worries.

Be careful with "monster spray"

Some families use pretend tools like "monster spray," and some children love them. Others find that pretending monsters exist makes the fear feel more real. Watch what works for your child.

Build a calming bedtime routine

A predictable routine helps children feel secure. Try the same steps every night: bath, pajamas, teeth, a story, a cuddle, and lights out. Keep the last hour before bed calm, with screens off and lights low. Avoid scary shows or exciting games close to bedtime.

Small changes in the bedroom

  • A night light. A soft, warm night light can make the room feel friendlier.
  • A comfort object. A favorite stuffed animal or blanket can be a "brave buddy."
  • Check the shadows together. Turn off the light and look around with your child, then turn it on to show what each shadow really is.
  • Leave the door open a crack if it helps, so they can hear familiar sounds.

Teach simple calming tools

Children can learn to calm their own bodies. Practice these during the day first, so they are easy at night.

  1. Balloon breathing. Breathe in slowly through the nose to fill your tummy like a balloon, then let the air out slowly.
  2. Happy place. Imagine a favorite place, like the beach or Grandma's kitchen, and describe it with all five senses.
  3. Brave words. Help your child pick a short phrase, like "I am safe and I am brave."
  4. Squeeze and relax. Squeeze hands into fists, count to five, then let go.

Read stories about being brave

Picture books are a gentle way to talk about fear. When a child sees a character who is scared and then finds courage, it helps them believe they can do it too. After the story, ask, "What helped the character feel brave? What could help you?"

I wrote Glimmer, the Night Light Who Was Afraid of the Dark for exactly this moment. It is about a little night light who is scared of the dark and learns to be brave. Many children find it funny that a night light could be afraid, and that makes their own fears feel a little smaller.

Praise brave steps

Notice and praise small successes: "You stayed in bed all night. That was brave!" Some families use a simple sticker chart for staying in bed. Keep rewards small and positive.

When to get extra help

Most bedtime fears fade over time. Talk with your pediatrician if your child's fears are very intense, last a long time, stop them from sleeping most nights, or show up during the day too. Frequent nightmares after a stressful event are also worth mentioning.

Be patient

Fears come and go. A child who has been fine for months may suddenly be scared again after a new experience. That is normal. With patience, comfort and a calm routine, most children learn that night is just another cozy part of the day.

Questions parents ask

Is it normal for kids to be afraid of the dark?

Yes. Fear of the dark is very common in young children, especially from about ages three to seven, when their imagination is growing quickly. Most children grow out of it.

Should I let my child sleep with the light on?

A soft, warm night light is a good middle ground. It helps many children feel safe without being too bright for sleep.

Should I let my scared child sleep in my bed?

Families make different choices. If you want your child to sleep in their own bed, staying close and calm, checking in briefly and using a comfort object can help them feel safe there.

When should I worry about bedtime fears?

Talk with your pediatrician if fears are very intense, last a long time, keep your child from sleeping most nights, or appear during the day as well.
