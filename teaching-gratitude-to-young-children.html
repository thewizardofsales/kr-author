Teaching gratitude to young children: simple ideas that stick

Easy, everyday ways to help kids ages 3 to 8 feel and show gratitude: thankful jars, thank-you notes, sharing activities, gratitude at the table and stories that help.

"Say thank you!" Most parents remind their children to do this many times a day. Saying the words is a good start, but real gratitude is a feeling: noticing the good things in our lives and the people who make them possible. It grows slowly, through small everyday moments. Here are simple, practical ways to help young children understand and practice gratitude.

Why gratitude matters for kids

Gratitude helps children notice what they have, not just what they want. It builds kindness and empathy because thankful children start to notice what others do for them. It also strengthens family relationships. Gratitude is not something children learn in one lesson. It grows through repetition, example and lots of little chances to practice.

Start with your own example

Children copy what they see. When they hear you thank the bus driver, tell your partner "thanks for making dinner," or say "I'm so grateful for this sunny day," they learn that gratitude is a normal part of life. Try saying out loud a few things you are thankful for each day.

Make gratitude part of the day

At the table

Go around the table at dinner and have everyone share one thing they are thankful for today. Keep it light. Answers like "the chocolate pudding" count. Over time, children often start to name people and experiences too.

At bedtime

As part of your bedtime routine, ask, "What was the best part of your day?" or "Who helped you today?" It ends the day on a warm note.

In the car

Play a quick game: take turns naming something you are thankful for that starts with each letter of the alphabet.

Hands-on gratitude activities

  1. Thankful jar. Keep a jar and some slips of paper. Whenever someone feels thankful, they write or draw it and drop it in. Read them together at the end of the month.
  2. Thankful tree. Draw a tree trunk on paper, or put a branch in a jar. Add a paper leaf for each thing your family is thankful for.
  3. Thank-you notes. Help your child draw a picture or write a few words to thank a teacher, grandparent or friend. Even a scribble with "Thank you" written by a grown-up means a lot.
  4. Gratitude walk. Go for a walk and point out things you are thankful for: a tree for shade, a friendly dog, a playground.
  5. Thank the helpers. Talk about the people who help your community, like mail carriers, firefighters and grocery workers, and make a card for one of them.

Connect gratitude with giving

Children understand gratitude better when they get to give, too. Let them help pick out food for a food drive, share a toy with a sibling, or bake cookies for a neighbor. Afterward, talk about how it felt to help someone else.

Use stories

Stories help children feel what gratitude is like, instead of just hearing about it. When a character shares, says thank you or realizes how lucky they are, children feel it with them. After reading, ask, "What was the character thankful for? What are you thankful for?"

My picture book Hazel's Thankful Basket tells the story of Hazel the squirrel, who shares her acorns with hungry friends and finds out what being thankful really means. It is a gentle way to start a conversation about sharing and gratitude, especially around Thanksgiving.

Keep expectations realistic

Young children are naturally focused on themselves, and that is normal for their age. A three-year-old may not feel deep gratitude yet, and that is okay. Keep practicing without lectures or guilt. Avoid saying things like "You should be grateful," which can make children feel bad instead of thankful. Instead, help them notice good things in the moment.

Celebrate the small moments

When you notice your child saying thank you without being asked, or helping someone, point it out: "I saw you thank your sister for sharing. That was kind." Noticing these moments helps them grow.

Gratitude is a lifelong habit. The small things you do together today, like a thankful jar, a thank-you drawing or a story at bedtime, plant seeds that can last a lifetime.

Questions parents ask

At what age can children understand gratitude?

Children as young as two or three can learn to say thank you, and by around ages five to eight many begin to understand the feeling behind it. Practice and example help it grow.

How can I teach my child to be thankful without lecturing?

Model gratitude yourself, make it part of daily routines like dinner and bedtime, and use hands-on activities such as a thankful jar, thank-you notes and stories.

What is a thankful jar?

A jar where family members drop slips of paper naming things they are thankful for. Reading them together later is a fun way to remember the good things in your life.

Are there picture books that teach gratitude?

Yes. Stories about sharing and thankfulness, like Hazel’s Thankful Basket, give children a gentle way to talk about gratitude.
